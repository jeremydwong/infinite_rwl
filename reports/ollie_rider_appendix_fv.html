Appendix 2: force–velocity limit on the legs (after R. McN. Alexander)

The rider model has one actuator per leg: a push-only force F acting along a telescoping strut from the foot to the rider's point mass. By default that force is bounded only by a constant, F ≤ Fmax = force_limit_bw·M g. That lets the optimiser push at full force while the leg is extending at any speed, which no muscle can do. This appendix adds an opt-in Hill-type force–velocity ceiling in the spirit of Alexander's jumping models (Alexander 1990, "Optimum take-off techniques for high and long jumps", Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 329:3–10; Alexander 1992, "A model of bipedal locomotion on compliant legs", Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 338:189–198; Alexander 1997, "Optimum muscle design for oscillatory activities", J. Theor. Biol. 184:253–259), all of which use the Hill (1938) hyperbola for the muscle.

From muscle to leg: why the curve lives on the leg

In a real leg the extensor muscles (hip and knee, plus ankle) act through moment arms rj on joints with angular velocities ωj; Alexander's convention is a single "equivalent muscle" whose shortening velocity vm and force Fm are related to the leg's axial extension rate v = dℓ/dt and axial force F through an effective gear ratio ρ(configuration): vm = ρ·v, F = ρ·Fm (power is conserved, Fmvm = F v). If ρ is treated as constant over the stroke — the assumption Alexander makes explicitly by lumping the leg into one muscle with one effective lever — the Hill hyperbola for the muscle maps to a Hill hyperbola for the leg with rescaled parameters Fmax = ρ Fm,0, vmax = vm,max/ρ, and the same dimensionless curvature k. So in the telescoping model the force–velocity law is a constraint on leg force versus leg extension rate; hip and knee are not separate actuators here.

On the question "you'd then be computing hip and joint work but maybe just assume equal parts hip and knee?": in this model the constraint is unaffected by that choice. Total leg work is ∫F v dt whatever the split. Assuming equal parts hip and knee is a cheap post-hoc attribution: it lets you quote hip work and knee work as ½∫F v dt each, and it is consistent with a constant-ρ two-link leg in which both joints extend in proportion. What it hides is (i) the real, configuration-dependent gear ratio — a bent knee near ℓ/ℓmax ≈ 0.6 has a much smaller effective moment arm than a nearly straight one, so the same leg force costs far more knee torque; (ii) joint-specific force–velocity limits (the knee extensors saturate at a different vm,max than the hip); and (iii) biarticular energy transfer. If any of those matter, the fix is a two-link leg with joint torques, not a different split of the same number.

The constraint as implemented

For each leg, at every force node k of every phase, with u the unit vector from foot to rider and v the leg extension rate,

v = (v_rider − v_foot) · u

F · (v_max + v / k)  ≤  F_max · (v_max − v)          (Hill, multiplied out — no division)
v  ≤  v_max
0  ≤  F  ≤  fv_eccentric_gain · F_max                 (variable bound)

For 0 ≤ v ≤ vmax the first line is exactly Hill's F ≤ Fmax(vmax − v)/(vmax + v/k), i.e. the form (F + a)(v + b) = (F0 + a)b with a/F0 = b/vmax = k. For v < 0 (leg compressing; the muscle is being stretched) the hyperbola rises above Fmax and the variable bound takes over — a flat eccentric plateau at fv_eccentric_gain·Fmax (default 1.0; Alexander used up to about 1.5–1.8 for eccentric force). For v ≤ −k vmax the left side is non-positive and the inequality is vacuous, so there is no singularity anywhere. The explicit v ≤ vmax line also applies to an unloaded leg (a muscle cannot shorten faster than vmax even at zero load); in practice it never binds because the feet stay on the board.

Foot velocities used for v: load phase — tail foot moves with the board, (vb, 0), and the front foot additionally slides along the deck at ds/dt ≈ (sk+1 − sk)/h (taken as zero on the last force node); pop phase — the board pivots about the rear wheel, so the COM velocity is (vb, −Xcω) and a deck point r has velocity vCOM + ω × r, plus the slide term rotated into the deck direction for the front foot; flight — board COM velocity (vb, vyb) + ω × r, plus the slide term. The force magnitude constrained in flight is the same one the reach complementarity gates: the cone/friction magnitude N when flight_force_dir is :cone or :friction, otherwise F. The same kinematics is reproduced by leg_extension_rates(sol) for reporting, and audit_rider_ollie now returns max_leg_extension_rate.

The unconstrained solution is the one the constant-force bound invites: the legs deliver two body weights each while extending at up to 17 m/s, giving a 1.5 m ollie. With the Hill ceiling the back leg rides the hyperbola (F/FHill ≈ 1) at about 1.7 m/s and 0.6 body weights, the pop lengthens from 0.06 s to 0.12 s, and the board's lowest point clears 0.20 m — in the range of a real ollie. The two runs are local optima from the same initial guess (the staged feasibility solve reports LOCALLY_INFEASIBLE at the fixed guess times with the limit on, then recovers in the warm-started stages), so the comparison is of solutions, not of global bounds.

Note on JuMP versus an "@equation" style

Alexander wrote his models as a handful of named equations solved by hand or by simple shooting. In the Julia/JuMP formulation the same statements are the macro calls that build the optimisation problem: @constraint(model, F*(vmax + v/k) <= Fmax*(vmax - v)) is the Hill line above, and @expression(model, v, (vxr - vfx)*ux + (vyr - vfy)*uy) would name the extension rate so that it can be reused in several constraints or in the objective. Since JuMP 1.x nonlinear expressions are written with ordinary operators inside @constraint/@expression (@NLconstraint is the legacy spelling); the helper fv!(F, ux, uy, vxr, vyr, vfx, vfy) in ollie_rider.jl is a small function that emits both constraint lines for a given leg and node, which plays the role of a reusable equation block.
