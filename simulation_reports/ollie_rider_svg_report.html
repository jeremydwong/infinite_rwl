Rider + board ollie: SMIL-animated SVG, objectives and actuator constraints

Model ollie_rider.jl (OllieRider). Three phases with free durations: load (flat board sliding on ice), pop (rear-wheel pivot, reduced coordinates), tail strike (Newton impact on the board only, e=0.18), flight, level landing. Rider is a point mass on two push-only telescoping legs; back foot on the tail tip, front foot free on the deck. Arrows show the force applied on the board at each foot (orange back/tail foot, green front foot), one shared scale: 0.5 length unit = 68.0 board weights = 2.0 rider body weights. Units: everything is nondimensional (board mass 1, g 1, ℓ_max 1); SI values use a 77.1 kg rider and ℓ_max = 0.9 m (board 2.27 kg, time unit 0.303 s, force unit 22.2 N).

Interpretation

Solve A lifts the board COM by 1.851 with the deck pitched to θ=0.152 rad at the apex, so its lowest board point (the rear wheel/tail side) reaches only 1.808; the tail-strike angle is 0.905 rad and the flight lasts 3.083. Solve B instead maximises the height of the lowest board point at the apex and reaches 1.804 (board COM rise 1.845, apex pitch 0.145 rad), i.e. it trades COM height for a level board in flight, which is what a real ollie is judged on. Solve B's lowest-point apex of 1.804 is 1.624 m above the ice with the rider at 1.914 m; flight lasts 0.919 s. The levelling is done with the flight cone: the front foot presses down on the nose (negative Fy on the board with a free horizontal component) while the back foot pulls its force to zero, so the pitch rate built by the tail strike is cancelled before the apex; on the ground both feet act along the legs and the pop is a back-foot dominated push (peak forces 67.997 and 67.989 board weights = 2.0 / 2.0 BW, at the 68.0 limit). In both solves the reach complementarity is what shapes the timing: a leg can only push while shorter than 1, so the rider extends the legs during the pop and then the board must rise into the feet during flight; the leg-slack panel shows where each leg goes slack. Constraint, impact, angular-NE and momentum audits sit at solver precision (max constraint violation 8.89e-06 / 5.76e-10, impact velocity error 9.93e-16 / 1.83e-15, angular NE residual 3.51e-07 / 3.51e-07, momentum drift 2.13e-14 / 1.42e-14), so the A/B differences are due to the objectives. The energy audit is NOT small: Σ(ΔE − W) = -8.55 / -8.478 ≈ 12.0% of the leg work, split -3.8 / -3.781 in load+pop and -4.75 / -4.697 in flight (where the legs do almost no work, W = -0.812). This is the first-order symplectic-Euler discretisation at 21/31/61 nodes, not a modelling error (the dynamics constraints are satisfied to 1e-8); the mesh-refinement check (residual must halve at 2× nodes) has not been run for this model yet. The horizontal drift in the animation is a visual offset of 0.0/s, not part of the dynamics (vx0=0).

Two facts about the solutions follow directly from this table. First, every optimum sits on the force bound for the whole load and pop phases (both legs at 50 during the crouch, the back leg at 50 through the pop), so the jump height and the board apex scale with the bound: it is the single most influential parameter in the model, ahead of restitution and foot placement. Second, the bound is a constant, independent of how bent the leg is and how fast it is extending. That is where it is least like a leg.

Why a constant force bound is the wrong shape

Following R. McN. Alexander's simplified jumping models (Optimum take-off techniques for high and long jumps, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 329, 1990; Leg design and jumping technique for humans, other vertebrates and insects, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 347, 1995; and the treatment in Principles of Animal Locomotion), the leg is a two-segment linkage driven by a knee-extensor muscle, and what is bounded is the joint torque, not the ground force. Three consequences for a point-mass-plus-telescoping-leg model like ours:

  1. Geometry converts torque to force. For a two-segment leg of equal segment lengths, with s = ℓ/ℓmax the fractional leg length, the moment arm of the leg force about the knee is κ(s) = ½√(1−s²) (in units of ℓmax; this is the same κ already used for the walking models' knee-torque cost). A torque limit τmax therefore gives a state-dependent force limit F ≤ τmax/κ(s): small in a deep crouch (s = 0.25: κ ≈ 0.48), unbounded at full extension (κ → 0). Our constant bound is the opposite: generous in the crouch, where the real leg is weakest, and restrictive near lock-out, where the real leg can carry large forces. Since both solutions here spend the whole load phase at s ≈ 0.25–0.4 with F = 50, the crouch is exactly where the present model is most optimistic.
  2. Force–velocity. Alexander's models give the extensor a Hill-type force–velocity relation: torque falls with joint angular velocity, reaching zero at a maximum shortening speed. The simplest usable form is τ ≤ τ0(1 − ω/ωmax) or, in leg-length terms, F ≤ (τ0/κ(s))·(1 − ℓ̇/vmax). The pop here extends the leg by ~0.7 ℓmax in ~0.2 s (ℓ̇ ≈ 3.5 ℓmax/s ≈ 3 m/s for a 0.9 m leg), which is far above the speeds at which a human extensor develops anything close to its isometric torque. Alexander's central result is that, because of this, the optimal jump technique and the achievable height are set by the force–velocity curve and by series elasticity (tendon recoil after a countermovement), not by peak isometric strength. Neither effect exists in a constant-bound model, and they act in opposite directions: force–velocity lowers the achievable pop, tendon recoil partly restores it.
  3. Magnitude. Non-dimensionalised by M g ℓmax, human peak isometric knee-extensor torque (~250–300 N·m for a 70 kg adult with ℓmax ≈ 0.9 m, M g ℓmax ≈ 620 N·m) is τ0 ≈ 0.4–0.5 M g ℓmax per leg. The present bound, F = 2 M g at s ≈ 0.25, implies a knee torque of F·κ ≈ 2·0.48 ≈ 1.0 M g ℓmax per leg — roughly 2–2.5× the isometric limit, and applied at extension speeds where the real muscle would deliver a fraction of isometric. This is the quantitative reason the jump heights here (rider rise ≈ 1.6–1.9 ℓmax, i.e. 1.4–1.7 m) are 3× a good human standing vertical jump (0.4–0.6 m), and why the board apex is correspondingly superhuman.

What can and cannot be rectified within the present framework

  • Directly rectifiable (drop-in constraint change): replace F ≤ Fmax by F·κ(s) ≤ τ0 per leg (κ from the current leg length, which is already a model expression), optionally with the linear force–velocity factor (1 − ℓ̇/vmax), where ℓ̇ is the leg-lengthening rate already used in the work term. This keeps the point-mass/telescoping abstraction and costs two constraints per node. Expected effects: the deep-crouch strategy disappears (weak at s = 0.25), the optimizer will start from a taller stance, the pop force will peak later in the extension, and jump and board heights fall toward human values. The flight-phase levelling pushes (a few board-weights) are far below any torque limit and would be unaffected, so the qualitative findings — the board levels at the apex, μ = 0 suffices — should survive; the absolute heights and flight times (~3 s) will not.
  • Rectifiable with one more state: tendon elasticity, which Alexander shows is essential for matching real jumps, needs a series-elastic element (a spring in the leg with its own length state) so that the countermovement in the load phase can store energy released during the pop. That is the same structure the walking models would need for elastic-leg gaits and is a modest extension.
  • Not rectifiable without changing the abstraction: (i) the two legs here are independent; a real rider shares one trunk, so hip torque and the rider's own rotation (ignored: the rider is a point mass) couple the legs — a torque limit on a real leg also bounds how fast the foot can be repositioned along the deck, which the free front-foot point s(t) currently does for free; (ii) the impulsive tail strike is modelled with a fixed restitution and is not force-limited at all; (iii) the rear-wheel reaction is unbounded, which is physically right for a rigid ground but means wheel/truck loads are not reported; (iv) the flight-phase foot forces assume a rigid, massless foot–deck contact, so any torque model bounds only their magnitude, not their timing.

Practical recommendation: keep the present constant bound as the "strength-unlimited" control case, add F·κ(s) ≤ τ0 with τ0 ≈ 0.45 M g ℓmax as the physiological case, and sweep τ0 the way μ was swept above. The result that matters scientifically — whether the ollie mechanism (pop, level in the air, land on the board without dragging) persists — is a yes/no question that this sweep answers cleanly; the heights are what change.

Comparison with the model

The model's I = 0.085 corresponds to 0.085·2.26·0.81 = 0.156 kg·m2, about 55 % higher than the 0.101 kg·m2 derived here. Part of the gap is the model geometry: its wheelbase (0.52 m) and deck (0.72 m) differ from the real 0.36 m / 0.81 m. Repeating the derivation with the model's geometry (rod 0.72 m, trucks at ±0.26 m) gives 0.050 + 0.075 + 0.002 = 0.127 kg·m2, i.e. I ≈ 0.069 nondimensional. So 0.085 is the right order of magnitude but on the high side either way.

Angular momentum used in the model. About the board COM, H = I ω with I = 0.055 (real geometry) in units of mbL2√(g/L). During the tail-on-ground phase the board pivots about the rear wheel: Ipivot = I + mbr2, where r is the COM-to-rear-axle distance, r2 = 0.1812 + 0.0312 = 0.0338 m2 (0.0417 nondim). Hence Ipivot = 0.101 + 2.262·0.0338 = 0.178 kg·m2, or 0.055 + 0.042 = 0.097 nondimensional (with the model's wheelbase, r2 = 0.262/0.81 = 0.083, Ipivot ≈ 0.15).

Recommendation

  • Board mass: 2.26 kg (deck 1.15 kg est. + 2 × 0.556 kg truck assemblies), matching the assumed 5 lb.
  • Mass ratio rider/board: 77.1/2.26 = 34.1; use rider_mass = 34.
  • Pitch inertia about COM: I = 0.055 nondimensional with the real deck/wheelbase; keep 0.07 if the current model geometry (wheelbase 0.58, deck 0.80) is retained. The existing 0.085 is ~25–55 % too high but not unreasonable given that grip tape, hardware and a heavier maple deck all push it upward.

Appendix 2: force–velocity limit on the legs (after R. McN. Alexander)

The rider model has one actuator per leg: a push-only force F acting along a telescoping strut from the foot to the rider's point mass. By default that force is bounded only by a constant, F ≤ Fmax = force_limit_bw·M g. That lets the optimiser push at full force while the leg is extending at any speed, which no muscle can do. This appendix adds an opt-in Hill-type force–velocity ceiling in the spirit of Alexander's jumping models (Alexander 1990, "Optimum take-off techniques for high and long jumps", Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 329:3–10; Alexander 1992, "A model of bipedal locomotion on compliant legs", Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 338:189–198; Alexander 1997, "Optimum muscle design for oscillatory activities", J. Theor. Biol. 184:253–259), all of which use the Hill (1938) hyperbola for the muscle.

From muscle to leg: why the curve lives on the leg

In a real leg the extensor muscles (hip and knee, plus ankle) act through moment arms rj on joints with angular velocities ωj; Alexander's convention is a single "equivalent muscle" whose shortening velocity vm and force Fm are related to the leg's axial extension rate v = dℓ/dt and axial force F through an effective gear ratio ρ(configuration): vm = ρ·v, F = ρ·Fm (power is conserved, Fmvm = F v). If ρ is treated as constant over the stroke — the assumption Alexander makes explicitly by lumping the leg into one muscle with one effective lever — the Hill hyperbola for the muscle maps to a Hill hyperbola for the leg with rescaled parameters Fmax = ρ Fm,0, vmax = vm,max/ρ, and the same dimensionless curvature k. So in the telescoping model the force–velocity law is a constraint on leg force versus leg extension rate; hip and knee are not separate actuators here.

On the question "you'd then be computing hip and joint work but maybe just assume equal parts hip and knee?": in this model the constraint is unaffected by that choice. Total leg work is ∫F v dt whatever the split. Assuming equal parts hip and knee is a cheap post-hoc attribution: it lets you quote hip work and knee work as ½∫F v dt each, and it is consistent with a constant-ρ two-link leg in which both joints extend in proportion. What it hides is (i) the real, configuration-dependent gear ratio — a bent knee near ℓ/ℓmax ≈ 0.6 has a much smaller effective moment arm than a nearly straight one, so the same leg force costs far more knee torque; (ii) joint-specific force–velocity limits (the knee extensors saturate at a different vm,max than the hip); and (iii) biarticular energy transfer. If any of those matter, the fix is a two-link leg with joint torques, not a different split of the same number.

The constraint as implemented

For each leg, at every force node k of every phase, with u the unit vector from foot to rider and v the leg extension rate,

v = (v_rider − v_foot) · u

F · (v_max + v / k)  ≤  F_max · (v_max − v)          (Hill, multiplied out — no division)
v  ≤  v_max
0  ≤  F  ≤  fv_eccentric_gain · F_max                 (variable bound)

For 0 ≤ v ≤ vmax the first line is exactly Hill's F ≤ Fmax(vmax − v)/(vmax + v/k), i.e. the form (F + a)(v + b) = (F0 + a)b with a/F0 = b/vmax = k. For v < 0 (leg compressing; the muscle is being stretched) the hyperbola rises above Fmax and the variable bound takes over — a flat eccentric plateau at fv_eccentric_gain·Fmax (default 1.0; Alexander used up to about 1.5–1.8 for eccentric force). For v ≤ −k vmax the left side is non-positive and the inequality is vacuous, so there is no singularity anywhere. The explicit v ≤ vmax line also applies to an unloaded leg (a muscle cannot shorten faster than vmax even at zero load); in practice it never binds because the feet stay on the board.

Foot velocities used for v: load phase — tail foot moves with the board, (vb, 0), and the front foot additionally slides along the deck at ds/dt ≈ (sk+1 − sk)/h (taken as zero on the last force node); pop phase — the board pivots about the rear wheel, so the COM velocity is (vb, −Xcω) and a deck point r has velocity vCOM + ω × r, plus the slide term rotated into the deck direction for the front foot; flight — board COM velocity (vb, vyb) + ω × r, plus the slide term. The force magnitude constrained in flight is the same one the reach complementarity gates: the cone/friction magnitude N when flight_force_dir is :cone or :friction, otherwise F. The same kinematics is reproduced by leg_extension_rates(sol) for reporting, and audit_rider_ollie now returns max_leg_extension_rate.

The unconstrained solution is the one the constant-force bound invites: the legs deliver two body weights each while extending at up to 17 m/s, giving a 1.5 m ollie. With the Hill ceiling the back leg rides the hyperbola (F/FHill ≈ 1) at about 1.7 m/s and 0.6 body weights, the pop lengthens from 0.06 s to 0.12 s, and the board's lowest point clears 0.20 m — in the range of a real ollie. The two runs are local optima from the same initial guess (the staged feasibility solve reports LOCALLY_INFEASIBLE at the fixed guess times with the limit on, then recovers in the warm-started stages), so the comparison is of solutions, not of global bounds.

Note on JuMP versus an "@equation" style

Alexander wrote his models as a handful of named equations solved by hand or by simple shooting. In the Julia/JuMP formulation the same statements are the macro calls that build the optimisation problem: @constraint(model, F*(vmax + v/k) <= Fmax*(vmax - v)) is the Hill line above, and @expression(model, v, (vxr - vfx)*ux + (vyr - vfy)*uy) would name the extension rate so that it can be reused in several constraints or in the objective. Since JuMP 1.x nonlinear expressions are written with ordinary operators inside @constraint/@expression (@NLconstraint is the legacy spelling); the helper fv!(F, ux, uy, vxr, vyr, vfx, vfy) in ollie_rider.jl is a small function that emits both constraint lines for a given leg and node, which plays the role of a reusable equation block.

Force–velocity headline: the appendix-2 test shows the unconstrained model's ollie depends on legs pushing at up to 2 BW while extending at 9–17 m/s; with a Hill limit (v_max 1.2 ≈ 3.6 m/s) the peak leg force drops to ~0.6 BW and the lowest-point apex falls from 1.46 m to 0.20 m — a realistic ollie height. The solves in this report are still run with force_velocity=false.
